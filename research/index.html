THE THREAD THROUGH MY WORK

Before I studied products,
I studied stories.

A good question can change
the whole story.

Documentary filmmaking taught me to stay curious when someone’s experience did not fit the story I expected. That habit still guides my research: listen carefully, look for the exception, and be willing to change the question.

My background spans communication, learning design, and UX research. In the Oak work, those interests meet in practical questions: does a requested feature address the barrier people described? Does an explanation of privacy answer a concern about control? What evidence would change the team’s next step? I like making those questions specific enough to investigate together.

I work across product research and design, and volunteer as a UX Research Lead at Oppia. Away from work, I run tabletop games. Facilitation, pacing, and leaving room for quieter voices matter there too. It is another place where listening changes what happens next.

How research moves with the team

Methods matter. So does making the next decision clear.

01 How do you help when requirements disagree?

In an Oppia lesson-player review, I flagged conflicting exit-dialog copy and button emphasis, then asked engineering to clarify when progress actually saves. I separated those open questions from recommendations about narrow layouts. That gave the team a focused set of decisions to resolve before implementation.

See how I connect evaluation to a team’s backlog
02 When would you change the research plan?

When the evidence challenges the premise. Oak’s privacy-community material raised concerns about control and company behavior, beyond understanding policy language. I used those themes to question whether deeper work with the same audience was the right next investment. The partial record supports that question, not a market-fit verdict.

See the research-direction decision
03 How do you keep findings useful without overstating them?

I separate the observation, my interpretation and the product decision it might inform. In the audience survey, reported barriers led to proposals for clearer steps, templates and reminders. Those proposals are visible alongside the sample limits; the survey does not prove that the proposed product changed behavior.
